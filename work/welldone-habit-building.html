WellDone

Making progress feel worth coming back to — exploring how a digital product can support habit building without turning one missed day into failure.

Role
UX/UI Designer

Product Design / Student Project
Focus
Habit Building, Behavior Change, Progress Visualization, UX/UI
01 — Overview

Overview

Starting a goal is usually not the hard part. Continuing it is.

WellDone explored how a digital product could support habit building without turning progress into another source of guilt. The project focused on making progress visible, creating small moments of positive reinforcement, and helping users see consistency over time.

02 — The Problem

When the streak becomes the punishment

Many habit applications are built around streaks. Streaks are motivating when everything is going well. They can also become punishing — miss one day and a month of progress can suddenly look like failure.

That creates a strange incentive structure where the product supposedly exists to support someone while simultaneously making them feel worse the moment real life interrupts the routine. WellDone explored a more forgiving approach.

03 — Designing Around Progress

Patterns over perfection

The experience focused on helping users see what they had accomplished rather than defining success entirely around perfection. Progress visualization became an important part of the product. Instead of reducing everything to "complete" or "failed," the interface could help people understand patterns over time.

04 — Building Motivation Into the Interface

Recognizing progress, not manipulating it

The product explored several principles from behavioral design: smaller achievable actions, visible progress, positive reinforcement, consistency over perfection, and clear next steps.

The intention was not to manipulate someone into opening the application. The interface needed to make the user's own progress easier to recognize.

05 — Making the Experience Feel Human

Between clinical and gamified

Habit products often become extremely clinical or extremely gamified. I wanted WellDone to sit somewhere between those extremes. The interface still needed structure — users needed to understand what they were working toward and what they had completed. But it also needed enough personality that the experience did not feel like filling out a productivity spreadsheet every day.

06 — Iterating the Experience

Finding the right amount of information

Wireframes and prototypes helped test how much information belonged in the primary experience. Too little information made progress feel meaningless. Too much made the product feel like analytics software.

The hierarchy therefore focused on giving users an understandable snapshot first, with more detailed information available when they wanted it.

07 — What I Learned

What the product teaches the user to feel

WellDone was an early lesson in the emotional side of interaction design. A product can technically function exactly as intended and still create the wrong experience. A habit tracker that accurately tells someone they broke a streak may be correct — that does not mean it is helping them build the habit.

The project helped push my design thinking away from did the user complete the interaction? and toward what is the product teaching the user to feel and do over time? That question has stayed with me in the professional products I have worked on since.
