01Overview

Designing for infrastructure thousands of miles away

WellDone is a nonprofit focused on improving access to safe drinking water in underserved communities through sustainable water infrastructure.

One of the challenges with installing water infrastructure is that the work doesn't end when a well or hand pump is installed. Infrastructure needs to continue functioning over time, and the people responsible for maintaining it need a way to understand when something may be wrong.

The project focused on interfaces supporting WellDone's remote well-monitoring system, including experiences for field workers and administrators as well as the supporting website.

My work centered on redesigning how sensor information, well status, maintenance needs, and related information were communicated so someone could understand what was happening without having to interpret a dense collection of raw information first.

02Understanding the Problem

Knowing a well exists isn't the same as knowing it's working

Remote water infrastructure creates an unusual UX problem.

The people monitoring a well may not physically be anywhere near it.

Administrators need enough information to understand what is happening across multiple wells, while workers in the field need information that helps them understand where attention may be required.

That means sensor data can't simply exist.

It has to communicate something useful.

The experience needed to help users answer basic questions quickly:

  • Which well am I looking at?
  • What is its current status?
  • When was the information last updated?
  • Is something wrong?
  • Does someone need to take action?
  • What information does a worker need in the field?
  • What information does an administrator need while monitoring multiple locations?

The existing interface made some of those answers harder to find than they needed to be.

03Research

Understanding the environment around the interface

A major part of the project was understanding that we weren't designing for a traditional office environment.

The system connected people responsible for monitoring water infrastructure with people who may be physically working around that infrastructure.

Those environments have very different constraints.

An administrator may need to monitor the status of multiple wells and quickly identify which ones require attention.

A field worker needs enough context to understand what is happening with a particular well and what needs attention without navigating through unnecessary information.

Connectivity was another major consideration.

The experience could not be designed around the assumption that everyone interacting with it would always have a reliable internet connection.

That pushed the design toward prioritizing information that could be understood quickly:

  • Current well status
  • Maintenance context
  • Alerts
  • Recent activity
  • Timestamps
  • Clear identification of the well or sensor
  • Communication between people monitoring and maintaining the infrastructure

The environment surrounding the product became just as important as the interface itself.

04Designing the Monitoring Experience

Status before data

The monitoring experience contained a lot of information, but not every piece of information had the same priority.

The interface needed to make the condition of the infrastructure understandable before asking someone to interpret the details behind it.

That led us toward a stronger hierarchy centered on status.

Instead of requiring users to scan through rows of information and determine which details mattered, the redesigned experience brought the most important information forward.

Well or sensor status needed to be immediately visible.

Timestamps also became important because sensor information without context about when it was collected could be misleading. Someone needed to understand not only what a sensor was reporting, but how current that information actually was.

Maintenance information and alerts could then provide additional context when something required attention.

05The Problem We Found

A table can contain everything and still tell you very little

One of the biggest issues with the monitoring experience was the way information was presented.

The existing approach relied heavily on dense tabular information.

Tables are useful when someone needs to compare exact values across a structured dataset, but they become harder to use when the primary question isn't What are all of these values?

The actual question was closer to Which of these wells needs my attention?

Users had to scan across information, interpret it, and mentally determine which entries were important.

The interface technically contained the information they needed, but it wasn't doing enough to communicate priority.

As we worked through the problem, it became clear that reorganizing the existing table wouldn't fully solve it.

The information architecture needed to change.

06The Pivot

From rows of data to scannable status

We moved away from relying on the table as the primary way to understand the system.

Instead, the redesign explored a card-based approach that allowed each well or monitored element to communicate its condition as a recognizable unit.

That changed the scanning behavior of the interface.

Rather than moving horizontally through rows and columns and interpreting several values before understanding what was happening, users could scan the page for status first.

The card structure gave us room to establish a clearer hierarchy:

Identity → Status → Recency → Maintenance Context → Additional Detail

This didn't mean the underlying information became less important.

It meant the interface stopped treating every piece of information as equally important.

07Designing for Different Responsibilities

The same infrastructure, different questions

The people interacting with the system weren't all doing the same job.

An administrator monitoring multiple wells needs a broader view. They need to understand which infrastructure is operating normally and where attention may be required.

A worker dealing with a particular well needs more focused context.

That meant the interfaces couldn't simply show the same information at different screen sizes.

The administrator experience emphasized monitoring, status visibility, alerts, and understanding what was happening across the system.

The worker experience needed to make information about the relevant well easier to understand in the field.

Maintenance context became especially important because identifying that something is wrong is only useful if the information can eventually support someone responding to the problem.

08Communication & Connectivity

Designing beyond the screen

A monitoring system only works if the information can eventually reach the person who can do something about it.

That made communication part of the UX problem.

We considered how alerts and maintenance information could move between the people monitoring infrastructure and workers responsible for responding in the field.

Phone-based communication and tools such as WhatsApp were relevant considerations because the most effective communication method isn't necessarily another notification buried inside a web application.

The same thinking applied to connectivity.

A workflow designed around constant high-speed internet access may work perfectly during a design review and fail in the environment where it actually needs to be used.

The product therefore had to prioritize clear, lightweight information and account for the reality that connectivity could be unreliable.

This was an important shift in how I approached the project.

We weren't just designing screens.

We were designing how information moved between people.

09Supporting the Larger Experience

Connecting monitoring with the organization behind it

The project extended beyond the individual monitoring interfaces.

The supporting WellDone website also needed to communicate the organization, its work, and the system surrounding the water infrastructure.

That meant thinking about the relationship between the public-facing experience and the tools used by the people actually monitoring and maintaining wells.

The visual and interaction design needed enough consistency that the experiences felt connected without pretending that every user had the same goal.

The website could explain the larger mission.

The administrator interface could support monitoring.

The worker experience could support field activity.

Together, those pieces formed a broader service rather than a collection of unrelated screens.

10Accessibility & Inclusive Design

Designing around actual constraints

Accessibility in this project extended beyond visual compliance.

The product had to account for people using different devices, working in different environments, and potentially dealing with unreliable connectivity.

Information hierarchy therefore mattered significantly.

Someone shouldn't need to read every value on the screen before understanding whether a well appears to be operating normally.

Status needed to be communicated clearly.

Timestamps needed to provide context.

Alerts needed to be distinguishable from normal information.

Navigation needed to stay focused enough that someone could understand where to go without learning a complicated system first.

The card-based direction helped by grouping related information and giving each monitored element a clearer structure.

Reducing the amount of interpretation required from the user became an accessibility decision as much as an information-design decision.

11Designing the System

Simplifying without oversimplifying

The redesign wasn't about removing technical information from the product.

That information existed for a reason.

The challenge was deciding when someone needed to see it.

Cards gave the interface a clearer first layer of information while still allowing more detailed information to exist when necessary.

That created a hierarchy between immediate status and deeper system information instead of forcing both into the same visual level.

The same principles needed to carry between the administrator experience, worker experience, mobile layouts, and supporting website.

The system had to remain recognizable even as the amount and type of information changed.

12What I Learned

The environment is part of the user experience

WellDone changed the way I thought about designing digital products for physical infrastructure.

It's easy to design an interface around the conditions in which you're designing it: a reliable computer, a good internet connection, plenty of time, and a comfortable environment.

Those aren't necessarily the conditions in which the product will be used.

The people interacting with WellDone's system could have completely different responsibilities, devices, connectivity, and proximity to the infrastructure itself.

That made context part of the design.

The biggest lesson for me was that simplifying an interface doesn't mean simplifying the problem.

Moving from a dense table toward scannable cards didn't remove the sensor information or the complexity behind monitoring water infrastructure. It changed the order in which the user had to understand it.

Instead of asking someone to interpret the system first, the interface could tell them what mattered first and let them investigate further when they needed to.

That idea has carried into a lot of my work since:

Show people what they need to understand first, then give them somewhere to go when they need the complexity underneath it.
