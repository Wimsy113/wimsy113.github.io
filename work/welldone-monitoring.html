Designing a Better Way to Monitor and Maintain Water Infrastructure

Redesigning how a nonprofit monitors remote water systems — from a dense table to scannable, status-first cards built for unreliable connectivity.

Role
UX Designer
Project Type
Client Project / Nonprofit
Focus
UX Research, Information Architecture, Interaction Design, Prototyping
01 — Overview

Overview

WellDone is a nonprofit organization focused on building and maintaining sustainable water infrastructure for communities that need reliable access to safe drinking water.

Providing the infrastructure itself is only part of that challenge. Once a well or water system is installed, someone still needs to know whether it is functioning correctly, when it requires maintenance, and what is happening across multiple locations. That becomes considerably more difficult when those locations are remote and internet connectivity is inconsistent.

Our project focused on improving the experience of monitoring wells and coordinating maintenance so that the people responsible for these systems could understand what needed attention without digging through an overwhelming amount of information.

WellDone project overview / hero image
02 — The Problem

Simple questions, buried in a dense table

WellDone needed to communicate information about water systems located across multiple communities. The existing experience presented much of that information through a dense, table-based interface. While tables can be useful when someone needs to compare large amounts of data, this implementation made it difficult to quickly understand the status of an individual well.

Users needed to answer much simpler questions:

  • Is this well working?
  • Does something need attention?
  • What happened?
  • What needs to happen next?

Those answers were buried inside an interface that required users to interpret multiple pieces of information before understanding the actual condition of a system. That was especially problematic because the product wasn't being designed for an environment where we could assume users would always have fast, reliable internet access.

The interface needed to make important information easy to identify and useful under less-than-ideal conditions.

03 — Understanding the Context

Not a dashboard for a desk

One of the biggest considerations throughout the project was understanding that we weren't designing a traditional dashboard for people sitting behind desks with reliable broadband connections. The product supported water infrastructure located in remote communities. That changed the way we thought about the experience.

Connectivity couldn't be treated as a guarantee. Information needed to be easy to scan. Important statuses needed to be obvious. The interface couldn't depend on users repeatedly navigating through several screens just to understand what was happening.

We had to think about what information mattered most when someone checked a well and how quickly we could help them understand its condition.

04 — From Tables to Cards

Access isn't the same thing as clarity

The existing table gave users access to information, but access wasn't the same thing as clarity. When everything is presented with similar visual weight, the user has to do the work of figuring out what matters. We wanted the interface to do more of that work for them.

Instead of asking users to read across rows of information and interpret the data themselves, we explored ways to organize the experience around the individual water systems being monitored. That led us toward a more visual, card-based approach.

Existing table-based interface

Moving from a dense table to cards was one of the most important changes in the project. Each card could represent a well or monitored location as its own object rather than simply another row in a database. That gave us space to establish a clearer hierarchy — the most important information could appear first, while secondary details could remain available without competing for attention.

A user scanning the page could quickly identify a well, understand its current condition, and determine whether something required attention. The goal wasn't to remove information. It was to make the information easier to process.

Early card concepts / wireframes
05 — Designing Around Status

A hierarchy built around what matters first

Once the information was separated into individual cards, we could think more intentionally about status. Monitoring data is only useful when someone can understand what it means. Rather than forcing users to interpret raw information before determining whether something was wrong, the design placed greater emphasis on communicating the state of the system itself.

This allowed someone checking several locations to scan the interface and focus their attention on the wells that needed it. The hierarchy became:

  • What am I looking at?
  • What is its current status?
  • Does it need attention?
  • What information do I need to understand the problem?

That hierarchy informed the way information was organized throughout the design.

Well card showing status hierarchy
06 — Supporting Maintenance

Connecting monitoring, alerts, and action

Monitoring a well doesn't accomplish much if the information can't lead to action. Maintenance therefore became an important part of how we thought about the experience. The product needed to help teams understand when something required attention and support the work that happened after a problem was identified.

Instead of treating monitoring, alerts, and maintenance as completely separate pieces of information, the experience needed to connect them. A person should be able to recognize that something was wrong, understand the available information about the issue, and determine what needed to happen next. This was especially important when coordinating work across multiple remote locations.

Maintenance workflow / task interface
07 — Designing for Limited Connectivity

Low connectivity wasn't an edge case

Low connectivity wasn't an edge case for this project. It was part of the environment we were designing for. That meant we couldn't build the experience around the assumption that users could constantly load new information or move through complicated navigation patterns.

We prioritized making important information visible and understandable with as little friction as possible. That influenced decisions around:

  • Reducing unnecessary navigation
  • Creating a strong information hierarchy
  • Making system status immediately visible
  • Keeping individual locations easy to identify
  • Avoiding unnecessary complexity
  • Considering how the experience would behave when connectivity was unreliable

The challenge wasn't simply designing a monitoring dashboard. It was designing one that still made sense in the environments where the work was actually happening.

08 — Monitoring and Communication

Making sense of a problem someone else has to solve

Another important part of the problem was communication. When infrastructure exists across remote locations, the information surrounding a maintenance issue may need to move between multiple people. Someone may identify the problem, while someone else is responsible for resolving it.

The experience therefore needed to communicate enough context for another person to understand what was happening without having to reconstruct the situation themselves. Clear statuses, maintenance information, and reporting all contributed to creating a shared understanding of the system.

Reporting / communication flow
09 — The Final Direction

Designed around what people are responsible for

The resulting direction moved away from treating the experience primarily as a collection of data. Instead, we designed around the things users were actually responsible for: the individual water systems and their condition.

Cards created clearer separation between locations. Status became easier to recognize. Maintenance information had greater context. And the overall experience required less interpretation before a user could understand what needed their attention.

10 — What I Learned

Context matters as much as the interface

WellDone reinforced something that has carried into a lot of my work since: context matters just as much as the interface.

It would have been easy to approach this as another dashboard redesign and focus entirely on making the data look cleaner. But the people using the product weren't necessarily sitting at desks with perfect internet connections. They were supporting physical infrastructure spread across communities where access to water mattered far more than whether a dashboard looked polished.

That changed the design priorities. The most successful solution wasn't the one that displayed the most information. It was the one that made the right information understandable when someone actually needed it. For me, that meant simplifying the experience without oversimplifying the work behind it.

Key Takeaway

Designing for real-world constraints means understanding what happens outside of the screen. For WellDone, improving the interface meant looking beyond the dashboard itself and considering connectivity, maintenance, communication, and the physical infrastructure the product existed to support.
