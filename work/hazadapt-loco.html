LoCo — Local Safety Information

Making local emergency information easier to find before it's needed — location-aware, multilingual, and built to still work when connectivity doesn't.

Role
Lead UX Designer
Platform
Web, Mobile Web
Focus
UX Design, Multilingual Experiences, Location-Aware Design, Accessibility, Mobile UX
01 — Overview

Overview

When an emergency happens, people often need information about a place immediately. The problem is that local emergency information tends to be scattered across city websites, county pages, PDFs, emergency-management systems, social media, and resources people may not even know exist.

HazAdapt's Local Safety Information product, commonly called LoCo, was designed to create a clearer way for people to access safety information associated with where they were.

My work focused on making that information understandable, accessible, multilingual, mobile-friendly, and resilient enough to remain useful when connectivity was unreliable.

02 — Designing Around Location

"I'm here. What do I need to know?"

Location is the foundation of LoCo. The application needed to determine which safety information was relevant to the user without expecting someone to understand government jurisdiction structures.

People think: I'm here. What do I need to know? They do not necessarily think: Which county emergency-management jurisdiction currently contains my coordinates? The interface therefore had to translate location data into something that felt natural.

Handling Boundaries

Geographic data becomes complicated around borders. Someone may be physically close to a community while technically falling just outside of its official boundary. We explored location buffering to reduce situations where tiny differences in coordinates produced dramatically different experiences.

The product also needed to represent multiple kinds of boundaries. We designed behavior around two boundary concepts: a boundary users could see in the interface, and a boundary associated with downloadable or location-specific content. That allowed the visual experience and underlying information behavior to serve different purposes without pretending they were always identical.

03 — Multilingual Safety Information

Accessibility means understanding it, too

Emergency information is only useful if people can understand it. LoCo was designed to support multiple languages, including English, Spanish, French, German, Russian, Chinese, and Japanese.

Language controls had to remain easy to find without overwhelming the core content. We treated language selection as part of accessibility rather than a secondary preference hidden deep inside settings.

04 — Mobile First Where It Matters

Designing for the device people actually have with them

People are unlikely to be sitting at a desktop computer when they suddenly need local emergency information. Mobile behavior therefore mattered significantly. LoCo moved into a mobile beta experience where we could test how location-aware safety information behaved on the device someone would most likely have with them.

Navigation included a "Jump to LoCo" interaction to get users into the local-information experience quickly. The goal was to reduce the distance between something is happening around me and here is information that can help me understand what to do.

05 — Designing for Bad Connectivity

When the network fails, the product can't

Emergency technology has an uncomfortable contradiction: the moment people need information most may also be the moment their internet connection works least reliably. LoCo therefore needed fallback behavior. We designed an offline experience so previously available or fallback safety information could still provide value when the application could not retrieve everything normally.

A roughly five-minute buffer also helped account for changes in location and connectivity instead of requiring constant perfect communication with the server.

06 — Content Hierarchy

Not just a collection of links

A safety-information product can easily become a giant collection of links. That was not what we wanted. Information needed to be structured around what someone was trying to understand. The interface therefore emphasized clear hierarchy, scannability, and the ability to move quickly between major categories of local information.

The design had to work for someone casually preparing at home and someone quickly looking for information during a stressful situation. Those are very different cognitive environments.

07 — Connecting LoCo to the Larger Platform

From public tool to shared signal

LoCo eventually became meaningful beyond the standalone public experience. Within ResiliencePoint, emergency-management professionals could investigate whether Local Safety Information was available within a particular area. That created a relationship between the public-facing preparedness tools and the analytics tools used by organizations.

One product helped people access local information. The other could help emergency managers better understand preparedness at the community level.

08 — What I Learned

Ideal conditions disappear — design for that

LoCo made location feel less like a map-design problem and more like an accessibility problem. A technically accurate boundary is not automatically a useful experience. A perfectly translated string is not automatically understandable. An application that works beautifully online is not automatically useful during an emergency.

Designing for emergency preparedness means constantly asking what happens when the ideal conditions disappear.
